Research journey / personal reflection / 2026

What I Learn from
Automation Technology
and Ocean Acidification Research.

An honest record of editorial rejection, extensive revisions, an inconclusive statistical test, and the eventual acceptance of my Automation manuscript.

Environmental manuscript: not acceptedAutomation manuscript: accepted by NHSJS

Not every project reaches publication. But I want to keep track of what each attempt taught me, including results I could not prove and feedback I did not expect.

01 / The first experience

A rejection is information, too.

My earlier manuscript, To What Extent Does Ocean Acidification Threaten the Marine Ecosystem, and What Can We Do About It?, was not accepted by the National High School Journal of Science (NHSJS). The editorial decision referred to conceptual advance, broader implications, and the fit of the work with the journal's aims and audience.

The letter did not identify specific methodological errors or say that the findings were invalid. I do not want to turn a general editorial decision into a technical critique it never provided. What I took from the experience was the importance of showing why a research question matters and what a study adds to existing understanding.

Lesson: A research topic can be interesting without making a sufficiently clear or original contribution for a particular journal. That distinction deserves attention early in a project.
02 / Taking it forward

Revision can reshape an entire study.

In my next project, I examined whether changes in industrial robot adoption were associated with Japan's ability to maintain manufacturing output despite declining labor input. My earlier submission experience gave me a better sense of what to consider from the beginning.

Yet the Automation manuscript still received substantial revision requests. In response, I expanded the literature review, added a theoretical framework and a clearer operational definition of compensation, revised the methodology, and moved toward manufacturing-specific employment, labor-hour, robot-density, and real value-added measures instead of broader mismatched indicators.

I also included a quantitative correlation test and strengthened the discussion of limitations and alternative explanations. This was more than editing sentences; it required rethinking how the analysis supported the research question.

Updated status: After revisions, the Automation manuscript was accepted for publication by NHSJS. The official journal article link and publication details have not yet been added.
Research milestone / 2026

An acceptance after revisions.

The Automation manuscript has now been accepted for publication by NHSJS. I want this archive to show both the outcome and the revisions and uncertainty that came before it. Acceptance is an important milestone, but it does not retroactively turn an exploratory correlation into proof of causation.

Publication distinction: Accepted for publication is not the same as published online. The journal link, formal citation, and other bibliographic details will be added when available.
03 / The numbers

A non-significant result is still a result.

The Automation study analyzed seven observation years between 1995 and 2023. A Pearson correlation between robot density and manufacturing labor productivity gave a positive coefficient, but not a statistically significant result at the conventional 0.05 threshold.

I cannot conclude that industrial robots caused Japan's changes in manufacturing output or productivity. The series has a small sample, uneven time intervals, and possible shared time trends. Increasing the number of years could improve the scope of a future analysis, but more observations alone will not resolve confounding factors or guarantee statistical significance.

Lesson: Statistical significance is not the goal by itself. The goal is a defensible analysis that communicates what the data support, what they do not support, and why.
04 / Next time

Four habits I want to build.

01

Check the data before committing to the question.

Look at coverage, sample size, definitions, gaps, and whether the units of analysis match.

02

Read prior work with a specific research gap in mind.

Ask what the literature already explains and what my own investigation could add.

03

Design the method around the claim.

Use appropriate comparisons and controls, especially when working with trends over time; distinguish correlation from causation.

04

Report uncertainty, not just encouraging numbers.

Preserve null results, limitations, alternative explanations, and the revisions that led to the final interpretation.

05 / Why archive this

Keep the process, not just the publication.

Part of why I created this website is that I enjoy investigating questions, but I also want a record of what I tried. The rejection of one manuscript and the acceptance of another are distinct outcomes, and neither should be confused with the public release of a final journal article. Each remains part of how I learn to do research.

“Research is not just about reaching an answer. It's about documenting how we got there.”

My archive is meant to preserve that process honestly.

Transparency note: This page is a personal reflection, not the published journal article. The Automation study is labeled as accepted, not yet published here, and its results remain exploratory rather than causal evidence.
